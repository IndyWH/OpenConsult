# 14 — Headache and "feeling run down": giant cell arteritis (UK private GP) — SUBTLE EMERGENCY

**Fictional patient:** Mrs. Margaret Ellison, 71, retired barrister
**Setting:** Private GP surgery, central London · **Speakers:** Doctor, Patient
**Target length:** 4–5 minutes
**Purpose:** urgency-escalation test case where the red flags are **buried in
casual, minimised asides** rather than volunteered as alarming symptoms. The
patient frames a new temporal headache as "getting old". The sight-threatening
features — jaw claudication, a transient loss of vision in one eye, scalp
tenderness — are each dropped in passing and waved away. The CDS must assemble
them into giant cell arteritis (temporal arteritis) and escalate, because
untreated GCA can cause **permanent, irreversible blindness within days**.
Deliberate contrast partner to script 13 (classic migraine): same presenting
complaint — headache — but this one is an emergency.

## Which evaluation this serves

- **Primary — CDS urgency evaluation (true positive).** The alarm must fire, and
  the test is whether it fires despite the patient's minimising tone and the red
  flags being scattered across the history.
- **Secondary — CDS clinical-content evaluation** (differential and plan) and the
  differential-restraint set as its **escalation** anchor: 11/12 must not fire,
  13 must not fire, 14 must.

## Expected clinical content (marking scheme for AI outputs)

- **Differential:** giant cell arteritis / temporal arteritis (leading —
  sight-threatening), with polymyalgia rheumatica overlap; alternatives to
  acknowledge and outrank: tension/other primary headache, trigeminal
  neuralgia — but the red-flag cluster makes GCA the working diagnosis until
  excluded.
- **Key positives (several deliberately buried):** new temporal headache for
  ~3 weeks in a woman over 50; **scalp tenderness** (mentioned as hurting to comb
  her hair / rest on the pillow); **jaw claudication** (jaw aches and tires while
  chewing, waved off as "my age"); a **transient episode of blurred / lost vision
  in one eye** that came and went (dismissed as "tired eyes"); morning shoulder
  and hip-girdle stiffness (PMR overlap); general malaise, low-grade feeling of
  being unwell, reduced appetite.
- **Key negatives:** no thunderclap onset, no neck stiffness/fever suggesting
  meningitis, no persistent focal limb weakness, no prior identical headaches.
- **Examination:** tender, thickened, poorly pulsatile temporal artery; scalp
  tenderness; visual acuity to be checked urgently; fundoscopy may be normal early.
- **Plan:** treat as GCA **immediately** — do not wait for tests or biopsy; start
  high-dose corticosteroid straight away (oral prednisolone 40–60 mg; because
  there has been visual involvement, this warrants urgent specialist input and
  possibly IV methylprednisolone); **same-day** urgent referral to
  ophthalmology / rheumatology / GCA fast-track pathway; urgent ESR and CRP;
  arrange temporal artery biopsy / ultrasound — but steroids first, biopsy does
  not delay treatment. Aspirin per specialist advice.

## Expected urgent_actions (marking scheme for the urgency evaluation)

- **Should fire:** **YES.** New temporal headache over 50 **with** jaw
  claudication **and** transient visual loss is GCA until proven otherwise — a
  sight-threatening emergency in which delay costs vision.
- **Expected content:** start high-dose corticosteroid immediately (do not await
  investigations); same-day emergency referral to ophthalmology/rheumatology
  (GCA pathway); urgent ESR and CRP; do not let biopsy scheduling delay steroids.
- **Expected first fire:** as soon as the buried cluster assembles — realistically
  when the transient monocular visual disturbance is mentioned alongside the jaw
  and scalp symptoms (mid-history), **not** left until the exam. The test is early
  assembly from casual asides.
- **Expected clearing:** the doctor starts steroids and arranges same-day
  specialist review near the end — the fired action should clear by the final
  update.

**DOCTOR:** Come in, Mrs. Ellison, have a seat. What can I help with today?

**PATIENT:** Oh, I feel a bit silly taking up your time, doctor. I've just had this headache that won't quite settle. I expect it's nothing — getting old, probably.

**DOCTOR:** Not silly at all. Tell me about the headache — where is it, how long have you had it?

**PATIENT:** It's here, mostly — the side of my head, the temple. About three weeks now. A dull, boring ache. I've never really been a headache person, so it's a bit unlike me.

**DOCTOR:** Is it there all the time, or does it come and go?

**PATIENT:** Most of the time, honestly. Some days worse than others. Paracetamol doesn't do a great deal.

**DOCTOR:** You said it's unlike you — you don't usually get headaches?

**PATIENT:** Almost never. That's partly why my daughter nagged me to come. She says I've been off.

**DOCTOR:** Off how?

**PATIENT:** Just run down. Tired. No appetite to speak of. And my shoulders and hips are terribly stiff in the mornings — takes me a good hour to get going. I put that down to the years, too.

**DOCTOR:** Let me ask about the head specifically. Is the scalp itself tender — when you brush your hair, or lie on the pillow?

**PATIENT:** Now you mention it — yes. Combing my hair on that side is horrible, I've started parting it the other way. Silly, isn't it. I thought I was just being delicate.

**DOCTOR:** Not silly. That's exactly the sort of thing I need to know. Any trouble with your jaw — does it ache or get tired when you're eating?

**PATIENT:** *(laughs)* Funny you say that — I had to give up on a steak last week, my jaw got so tired chewing I had to stop. I said to my husband, that's it, I'm officially ancient.

**DOCTOR:** And your vision — any changes at all? Even something brief?

**PATIENT:** ...Well. There was one odd moment. A few days ago, my right eye went — sort of dim, like a shade coming down, for a minute or two. Then it cleared. I assumed I was just tired, I'd been reading. It hasn't happened since, so I didn't think it worth mentioning.

**DOCTOR:** I'm very glad you did mention it. That is worth mentioning. Was it one eye or both?

**PATIENT:** Just the right. The one the headache's worse on.

**DOCTOR:** Any double vision, or has anything like that dimming happened again since?

**PATIENT:** No, just the once. It frightened me a little at the time, then it passed and I forgot about it.

**DOCTOR:** Let me examine you now — I want to feel along your temples. ... The artery here on the right, at your temple, is tender when I press, and it feels firm and cord-like, and I can't feel a normal pulse in it the way I can on the left. Your scalp there is tender too. Let me check your vision quickly.

**PATIENT:** Is something wrong, doctor? You've gone rather serious.

**DOCTOR:** I'm going to be straight with you, Mrs. Ellison, because this matters and there's no time to dress it up. I'm concerned you have a condition called giant cell arteritis — it's inflammation of the arteries at the temple. On its own the headache would be one thing, but the jaw tiring when you chew, the tender scalp, and above all that episode where your vision dimmed — those together point to it, and none of them are "getting old".

**PATIENT:** That sounds serious.

**DOCTOR:** It's treatable, and treatable well — but it's urgent, because if it's left, it can damage the sight in that eye permanently, and that transient dimming is a warning shot. So we don't wait. I'm going to start you on a high dose of steroid tablets right now, today, before any test comes back — because the treatment is what protects your vision, and we don't delay it for paperwork.

**PATIENT:** Right now? Before you even know for sure?

**DOCTOR:** Yes — deliberately. With this one, we treat first and confirm after, because the cost of waiting is your eyesight. I'm also referring you to the eye specialists and the rheumatology team on a same-day urgent basis — I'll call ahead while you're here. And I'm sending you for urgent blood tests, two inflammation markers, today. There's a small artery biopsy they'll arrange to confirm it, but that happens *around* the treatment, it doesn't hold it up.

**PATIENT:** Goodness. And I nearly didn't come.

**DOCTOR:** You came, and you told me about the eye — that's what matters. Take the first steroid dose here before you leave. If at any point between now and being seen your vision dims again, or goes and doesn't come back, you don't wait for the appointment — you go straight to the eye casualty or call 999.

**PATIENT:** I will. Thank you for taking it seriously.

**DOCTOR:** Let me make that call now and get your first dose. Stay where you are.
