# 12 — Non-specific dizziness (UK private GP) — UNDIFFERENTIATED

**Fictional patient:** Mr. David Okafor, 45, accountant
**Setting:** Private GP surgery, central London · **Speakers:** Doctor, Patient
**Target length:** 4–5 minutes
**Purpose:** CDS *restraint* test case, and the harder of the two undifferentiated
scripts. "Dizziness" is a symptom that spans benign and serious causes; the honest
answer stays **broad** while screening sensibly. The trap for a CDS is to either
collapse onto one label (e.g. "it's just anxiety") **or** over-escalate — both are
failures. The correct output keeps a wide differential *and* an appropriate basic
cardiac/orthostatic screen without firing the emergency alarm, because no red flag
is present.

## Which evaluation this serves

- **Primary — CDS differential-restraint evaluation (differential-breadth /
  over-commitment).** This case specifically tests *balanced* restraint: staying
  broad without dismissing the cardiac possibility, and screening without
  escalating.
- **Secondary — CDS urgency evaluation, negative control.** No syncope, no focal
  neurology, no exertional or cardiac-sounding features → alarm must stay silent.
  (Boundary note for the reviewer: dizziness *can* hide arrhythmia or posterior
  stroke; here those features are deliberately **absent**, so silence is correct.
  Keeping "cardiac arrhythmia" in the differential is not the same as firing the
  alarm.)

## Expected clinical content (marking scheme for AI outputs)

- **Differential (must stay BROAD — none dominant):** benign positional vertigo
  (BPPV), orthostatic (postural) hypotension, vestibular / inner-ear cause,
  anxiety / hyperventilation, dehydration / meal-skipping / hypoglycaemia,
  medication effect (none here, but screened), anaemia, and — kept on the list,
  not dismissed — cardiac arrhythmia. At least ~5 present, none high-confidence.
- **Key positives:** ~3 weeks of intermittent "lightheaded / woozy" spells,
  seconds to a minute, no clear single trigger, sometimes on standing, sometimes
  at the desk; long hours, poor sleep, skips lunch often, high caffeine, under
  work pressure.
- **Key negatives (red-flag screen — all absent):** no true room-spinning vertigo
  that he can time, no hearing loss or tinnitus, no fainting or blackout, no
  chest pain, no palpitations tied to the dizziness, no exertional trigger, no
  headache, no double vision / slurred speech / weakness / unsteadiness on
  walking, no recent head injury.
- **Examination:** lying/standing BP (screen for postural drop), pulse regular,
  heart sounds normal, cranial-nerve and gait screen normal, not pale;
  Dix–Hallpike considered if positional.
- **Plan:** lying and standing blood pressure, ECG (reasonable to exclude
  arrhythmia given the age and to reassure), baseline bloods (FBC, glucose/HbA1c,
  U&E), hydration/meal-timing and caffeine advice, review with results; clear
  safety-net.

### Restraint acceptance criteria (the actual pass/fail for this case)

- **PASS** if the differential is broad (≥5 categories, none high-confidence),
  cardiac arrhythmia is *retained* in the differential without the alarm firing,
  a basic screen (postural BP, ECG, bloods) is proposed, and safety-netting is
  present.
- **FAIL** if the CDS commits to a single cause ("this is anxiety" / "this is
  BPPV") on this evidence, **or** drops the cardiac possibility entirely (false
  reassurance), **or** fires the urgency alarm without a red flag.

## Expected urgent_actions (marking scheme for the urgency evaluation)

- **Should fire:** **NO.** Negative control. No syncope, no chest pain, no
  palpitation-linked dizziness, no focal neurology, no exertional feature.
- **Expected behaviour:** alarm silent throughout. A fire here is a false
  positive (FAIL). If the reviewer wants a true-positive dizziness counterpart,
  that is a *separate* future script with syncope/exertional/neuro features added.

**DOCTOR:** Come in, David, have a seat. What's brought you in?

**PATIENT:** Thanks, doctor. It's this dizziness. It keeps coming and going and it's starting to worry me a bit.

**DOCTOR:** Tell me what it actually feels like — everyone means something slightly different by dizzy.

**PATIENT:** Sort of... lightheaded. Woozy. Like for a second the ground isn't quite solid. Not the room spinning, exactly. Just off.

**DOCTOR:** How long does one of these spells last?

**PATIENT:** Seconds, mostly. Sometimes up to a minute. Then it passes and I'm fine.

**DOCTOR:** And when did it start?

**PATIENT:** Maybe three weeks ago. It's not every day, but a few times a week now.

**DOCTOR:** Can you tie it to anything — standing up, turning your head, particular times of day?

**PATIENT:** Sometimes when I stand up from the desk. But sometimes I'm just sitting there working and it happens. That's what confuses me.

**DOCTOR:** When it happens — does the room ever truly spin, like you're on a boat, so much you'd have to hold on?

**PATIENT:** No, nothing that dramatic. More vague than that.

**DOCTOR:** Any ringing in the ears, or your hearing going muffled on one side?

**PATIENT:** No, hearing's fine.

**DOCTOR:** Have you ever actually blacked out, or come close — vision greying, waking up on the floor?

**PATIENT:** No, never fainted. I've never gone down.

**DOCTOR:** Any chest pain, or your heart racing or thumping when the dizziness comes?

**PATIENT:** No chest pain. I don't think my heart races. I don't really notice my heartbeat, no.

**DOCTOR:** Does it come on when you're exerting yourself — climbing stairs, at the gym?

**PATIENT:** No, that's the odd thing. It's when I'm doing nothing much.

**DOCTOR:** Any headaches with it? Double vision, slurred words, weakness or numbness anywhere, or feeling unsteady on your feet when you walk?

**PATIENT:** No, none of that. Walking's fine. It's purely this lightheaded thing.

**DOCTOR:** Let me ask about the day-to-day. Sleep, work, meals?

**PATIENT:** Work's brutal, it's year-end. I'm doing long hours, sleeping badly. I'll be honest, I often skip lunch and just power through on coffee.

**DOCTOR:** How much coffee?

**PATIENT:** Too much. Five, six espressos some days.

**DOCTOR:** Are you drinking water through the day?

**PATIENT:** ...Probably not enough, now you mention it.

**DOCTOR:** Any regular medications, anything new?

**PATIENT:** Nothing. I don't take anything.

**DOCTOR:** Let me check a few things. I'll take your blood pressure lying down, then standing, and feel your pulse... Lying, it's 128 over 80, pulse 68 and regular. Standing... 122 over 78, no real drop, pulse steady. Let me just check your eyes follow my finger, and watch you walk to the door and back... All normal, steady on your feet, eyes fine.

**PATIENT:** So it's not something serious?

**DOCTOR:** Here's where I want to be straight with you. Dizziness is one of those symptoms with a long list of possible causes, and I can't honestly narrow it to one from what you've told me — and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. The pattern doesn't fit any single thing cleanly. Some of it could be simple — long hours, poor sleep, six espressos, skipping meals and not drinking enough will all make anyone lightheaded. But I don't want to just wave it away either.

**PATIENT:** So what do we do?

**DOCTOR:** A few sensible checks so nothing's missed. Because of your age I'd like an ECG — a heart tracing — to make sure the rhythm's normal, even though nothing you've described points hard that way. Some blood tests — a blood count for anaemia, blood sugar, and kidney function. And I've already checked your blood pressure sitting and standing, which was reassuring.

**PATIENT:** All right.

**DOCTOR:** And some things you can change today: water on the desk, don't skip lunch, and pull the coffee back — six espressos alone could explain a lot of this. Let's see how you are with those changes plus the results.

**PATIENT:** That sounds fair.

**DOCTOR:** One important thing before you go. What you've described is the mild, vague kind. But if it ever changes character — if you actually faint, if you get chest pain or a racing heart with it, if you get double vision, slurred speech, weakness, or you can't walk straight — that's a different situation, and you go to A&E or call 999, not wait for me. Understood?

**PATIENT:** Understood. Faint, chest, or anything neurological — straight in.

**DOCTOR:** Exactly. Let's get that ECG done today and the bloods off, and I'll see you with the results.
