# 05 — Epigastric pain (English)

**Fictional patient:** Mr. Sunil Bandara, 35, delivery driver
**Speakers:** Doctor, Patient · **Target length:** 3–4 minutes

## Expected clinical content (marking scheme for AI outputs)

- **Differential:** peptic ulcer disease / NSAID-related gastritis (leading), GORD, functional dyspepsia
- **Key positives:** burning epigastric pain ~6 weeks, worse when hungry and at night, relieved by food and antacids, regular ibuprofen for back pain (several times a week), arrack most weekends, irregular meals with long gaps, high job stress
- **Key negatives (alarm screen):** no weight loss, no difficulty or pain swallowing, no vomiting blood, no black tarry stools, no persistent vomiting, appetite preserved, no previous ulcer
- **Examination:** mild epigastric tenderness only, no masses, no guarding, not pale
- **Plan:** stop ibuprofen completely (paracetamol for the back instead), omeprazole 20 mg daily before breakfast for 4 weeks, H. pylori stool antigen test, reduce alcohol, regular meals, review in 2 weeks with result; safety-netting — immediate hospital if vomiting blood or black stools; endoscopy referral if not settling or any alarm feature appears

---

**DOCTOR:** Good evening. Have a seat. What's the trouble?

**PATIENT:** Good evening, doctor. It's my stomach. A burning pain, right here, top of the stomach. It's been going on about six weeks.

**DOCTOR:** Show me exactly where... okay, just below the chest, in the middle. What kind of burning?

**PATIENT:** Like a fire inside, doctor. Sometimes it gnaws, like hunger but painful.

**DOCTOR:** When is it worst?

**PATIENT:** When I'm hungry, mostly. And at night — around one or two in the morning it wakes me sometimes. If I eat something or take a Gelusil, it settles.

**DOCTOR:** So food helps. Any pain going through to the back? Any spreading up the chest?

**PATIENT:** Not to the back. A little burning up the chest sometimes after late meals.

**DOCTOR:** Now some important questions. Have you lost weight recently without trying?

**PATIENT:** No, doctor. If anything the wife says I've put on.

**DOCTOR:** Appetite normal? Any difficulty swallowing — food getting stuck, pain on swallowing?

**PATIENT:** Appetite is fine. No trouble swallowing.

**DOCTOR:** Any vomiting? Ever vomited blood, or anything looking like coffee grounds?

**PATIENT:** No vomiting, no blood, nothing like that.

**DOCTOR:** And your motions — colour normal? Ever black, like tar?

**PATIENT:** Normal, doctor. Nothing black.

**DOCTOR:** Good — those were my warning-sign questions, and you've passed all of them. Now, do you take any medicines regularly? Painkillers?

**PATIENT:** Ah, yes. For my back — with the driving, the back aches — I take ibuprofen. Maybe three, four days a week I'd say. Sometimes more.

**DOCTOR:** For how long has that been going on?

**PATIENT:** Months, doctor. Over a year, on and off.

**DOCTOR:** That's very likely part of the problem — I'll come back to it. Alcohol?

**PATIENT:** Weekends. Arrack, with the friends. Saturday, sometimes Friday also.

**DOCTOR:** How much on a night?

**PATIENT:** Quarter bottle... sometimes a bit more, honestly.

**DOCTOR:** Smoking?

**PATIENT:** No, never took to it.

**DOCTOR:** Meals — regular?

**PATIENT:** *(laughs)* Doctor, with delivery work? Morning tea and a bun, then sometimes nothing till evening. Lunch happens if there's time.

**DOCTOR:** Long empty stomach, painkillers, weekend arrack — the picture is forming. Any big stress?

**PATIENT:** The job, doctor. Targets, traffic. It's not easy.

**DOCTOR:** Understood. Lie down on the bed, let me examine your tummy. Relax the muscles... breathing normally... any pain here?... here?

**PATIENT:** There, doctor — that spot is tender.

**DOCTOR:** Mild tenderness just at the top of the stomach, nothing else — no lumps, the rest of the tummy is soft. Let me see your eyes... you're not pale, so no sign of blood loss. Sit up.

**PATIENT:** Is it serious, doctor?

**DOCTOR:** The pattern fits acid damage — likely irritation or an ulcer forming in the stomach lining. The ibuprofen is the biggest culprit; it strips the stomach's protective layer. The empty stomach, the arrack, and the stress all pile on top. The good news: no warning signs, and this usually heals well with treatment.

**PATIENT:** What do we do?

**DOCTOR:** Five things. One — the ibuprofen stops today, completely. For the back, use paracetamol; it doesn't harm the stomach. We can talk properly about the back another day. Two — I'm starting omeprazole, one in the morning, half an hour BEFORE breakfast, for four weeks. It switches off the acid and lets the lining heal. Three — a stool test for a germ called H. pylori, which lives in the stomach and causes ulcers. If it's positive, we add a course of antibiotics. Four — regular meals. Something every three to four hours, even a banana in the vehicle. And bring the arrack down — this is the moment. Five — come back in two weeks with the stool result.

**PATIENT:** Before breakfast, the tablet. Okay, doctor.

**DOCTOR:** And the same warning signs I asked about: if you ever vomit blood, or the motions turn black like tar — that's not a clinic visit, that's the hospital, immediately, even at night. If the pain hasn't settled after the four weeks, or any of those signs appear, we'll send you for an endoscopy — the camera test — to look directly.

**PATIENT:** Hospital if blood. Got it, doctor.

**DOCTOR:** Good. Two weeks, with the stool report. And seriously — no more ibuprofen, even if the back complains.

**PATIENT:** Understood. Thank you, doctor.
