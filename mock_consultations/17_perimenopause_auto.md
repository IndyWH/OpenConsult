**Fictional patient:** Ms Sarah Whitfield, 48, deputy head teacher
**Setting:** Private GP surgery, central London · **Speakers:** Patient (auto mode — Alba asks, the doctor supervises)
**Format:** auto-mode actor brief — a read-aloud golden-minutes script, then a background paragraph to answer from
**Target golden window:** `AUTO_GOLDEN_MINUTES_S`, default 90 s · golden script ≈ 215 words
**Purpose:** the everyday presentation the whole set is missing — a middle-aged
woman arriving with a scatter of symptoms she has not connected to each other.
Broken sleep, night sweats, erratic heavy periods, low mood, word-finding
difficulty, aching joints and exhaustion are delivered as a list of separate
complaints, in the order they bother her rather than in any diagnostic order.
Nothing is buried and nothing is minimised; the work is assembly. She was offered
antidepressants eighteen months ago and declined them, which is the commonest
real-world failure in this presentation and is written into the script so the
consultation has to do better than that. It is also a restraint case in the
opposite direction from the alarm cases: the picture is strong enough to name,
but two treatable conditions — thyroid disease and iron deficiency from heavy
bleeding — must stay on the list rather than be crowded out.

## Which evaluation this serves

- **Primary — Phase 7c interview conduct.** Elicitation coverage across an
  undifferentiated multi-system history, and the open-to-closed cone on a patient
  who gives a great deal in the golden minutes and then needs systematic
  follow-up. The script contains a genuine mid-narrative dry-up, so the
  encouragers are exercised where a real patient would falter.
- **Secondary — CDS restraint and calibration.** Perimenopause should lead, but
  hypothyroidism, iron deficiency anaemia and a mood disorder must survive on the
  differential. An output that names only the menopause has over-committed; an
  output that will not name it has under-committed.
- **Tertiary — an omission test.** Past migraine with aura is the single fact
  that changes the safe route for hormone replacement, and she will not volunteer
  it.

## Background — answer from this when Alba asks

The symptoms have come on gradually over twelve to eighteen months. She has
drenching night sweats four or five nights a week and six to eight hot flushes
during the day, which are worst at work and have twice made her step out of a
lesson observation. Her last period was five weeks ago and the one before that
came after a three-week gap; on the second day she floods, uses two forms of
protection and has had to leave work once because of it. She has had no bleeding
between periods and none after sex, and her smear eighteen months ago was normal.
Her mood is irritable and tearful with a flat sense of dread in the mornings; she
still enjoys things when she gets to them, has no thoughts of harming herself,
and is clear in her own mind that this is not depression. The cognitive
symptoms — losing words, forgetting names, losing the thread mid-sentence — are
worse when she is tired and are what frighten her most. Her fingers and knees are
stiff for twenty to thirty minutes each morning with no swelling or redness. If
she is asked directly, she has vaginal dryness, sex is uncomfortable, she has
urinary urgency and she has had two urine infections in the past year. **In her
twenties she had migraines with visual aura**, which stopped in her thirties and
which she regards as ancient history and will not raise herself. She has no other
medical history, takes nothing regularly apart from occasional ibuprofen, has no
allergies, and needs no contraception because her husband had a vasectomy eight
years ago. Her mother had an underactive thyroid and went through the menopause
at fifty; her maternal grandmother broke a hip at seventy; there is no breast
cancer in the family. She is a deputy head with two children aged fourteen and
eleven, drinks a glass of wine on four evenings a week and rather more lately,
has never smoked, and stopped running about a year ago. She has put on around five
kilograms in the past year, mostly around her middle. She thinks it may be her
thyroid because of her mother. If she is asked what she is frightened of, and
only then, she says she has been reading about early dementia. She wants to know
whether this is the menopause and whether anything can be done, and she has read
contradictory things about HRT and breast cancer and does not know what to
believe.

## Expected clinical content (marking scheme for AI outputs)

- **Differential:** perimenopause — leading, and it should be named rather than
  described. Also correctly on the list: hypothyroidism (family history,
  overlapping symptoms), iron deficiency anaemia secondary to heavy menstrual
  bleeding, depressive or anxiety disorder, primary sleep disorder; diabetes as a
  lesser possibility. **Restraint point:** an output that narrows to the
  menopause alone and drops thyroid and anaemia has over-committed, because both
  are common, both are treatable in their own right, and heavy bleeding makes
  iron deficiency likely on its own.
- **Key positives:** age 48; vasomotor symptoms day and night; cycle length
  varying from three to seven weeks with menorrhagia; mood lability and morning
  anxiety; word-finding and memory difficulty; arthralgia with short-lived
  morning stiffness; vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, urinary urgency and recurrent
  urinary infection; fatigue; weight gain; twelve to eighteen months' duration.
- **Key negatives that earn their place:** no intermenstrual or postcoital
  bleeding, smear up to date and normal, no joint swelling or redness, no
  suicidal ideation, no neck swelling or dysphagia.
- **Background that must be elicited:** **migraine with aura** (she will not
  offer it, and it steers the hormone replacement discussion towards a
  transdermal route); the family history of thyroid disease and of fragility
  fracture; the rising alcohol intake; and the dementia fear, which will not
  surface unless someone asks what she is worried about.
- **Examination:** blood pressure, weight and body mass index, thyroid
  examination; consider abdominal and pelvic examination in view of the heavy
  bleeding.
- **Investigations:** full blood count and ferritin, and thyroid function. **FSH
  is not required** to diagnose perimenopause in a woman over 45 with typical
  symptoms, and an output that orders it reflexively should be marked down;
  saying so explicitly is a mark in favour. An age-appropriate check of HbA1c and
  lipids is reasonable.
- **Plan:** name the perimenopause and explain it in plain words, because being
  given the name is most of what she came for. Discuss hormone replacement
  including the past migraine with aura and the transdermal route, with a
  progestogen for endometrial protection since she has a uterus. Treat the
  genitourinary symptoms separately with vaginal oestrogen, which is not the same
  conversation as systemic HRT. Address the heavy bleeding on its own terms
  rather than as a footnote. Cover sleep, alcohol, exercise and bone health.
  Answer the dementia fear directly. Review at three months.

## Expected urgent_actions (marking scheme for the urgency evaluation)

- **Should fire:** **NO.** This is a negative control. Nothing here is
  time-critical, and heavy but regular-pattern bleeding in a woman with a normal
  recent smear and no intermenstrual or postcoital bleeding does not meet an
  urgent referral threshold.
- **If it fires:** record what triggered it. The plausible candidates are the
  menorrhagia and the cognitive symptoms; an alarm on either would show the
  threshold sitting too low for ordinary general practice.
