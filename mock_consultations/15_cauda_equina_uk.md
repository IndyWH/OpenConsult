# 15 — Back pain after lifting: cauda equina syndrome (UK private GP) — SUBTLE EMERGENCY

**Fictional patient:** Mr. Tom Bradley, 40, personal trainer
**Setting:** Private GP surgery, central London · **Speakers:** Doctor, Patient
**Target length:** 4–5 minutes
**Purpose:** urgency-escalation test case in which a time-critical emergency is
hidden inside the single most common, most benign-sounding presentation in general
practice — mechanical low back pain after lifting. The red flags of cauda equina
syndrome (saddle numbness, bladder dysfunction, bilateral leg symptoms) are each
dropped as embarrassed, throwaway asides and immediately minimised by the patient.
The CDS must extract them from ordinary back-pain noise and escalate, because
delayed decompression of cauda equina risks **permanent bladder, bowel and sexual
dysfunction**. This is the "buried red flag" test at its hardest: the base rate of
back pain is enormous and almost all of it is benign.

## Which evaluation this serves

- **Primary — CDS urgency evaluation (true positive).** The alarm must fire, and
  the test is whether it fires when the red flags arrive as casual, minimised
  mentions amid a classic mechanical-back-pain history — i.e. resistance to the
  high benign base rate.
- **Secondary — CDS clinical-content evaluation** (differential and plan) and the
  restraint set's second **escalation** anchor alongside 14.

## Expected clinical content (marking scheme for AI outputs)

- **Differential:** cauda equina syndrome (leading — surgical emergency) arising
  on a background of acute lumbar disc prolapse with sciatica; the mechanical back
  pain is real but is **not** the safe end-point once the red flags appear.
- **Key positives (deliberately buried and minimised):** acute low back pain ~1
  week after heavy deadlifting, with pain radiating down a leg (sciatica);
  **saddle/perineal numbness** (noticed as reduced sensation "when I wipe",
  embarrassedly); **urinary difficulty** — hesitancy, weak stream, having to
  strain, sensation of not emptying (put down to "drinking less water"); **bilateral
  leg symptoms** — pins-and-needles now in both feet, not just the sciatic leg;
  symptoms progressing over days.
- **Key negatives:** no fever (less likely infective/abscess), no trauma beyond
  the lift, no history of cancer volunteered.
- **Examination:** assess for reduced perianal/saddle sensation and anal tone,
  bilateral lower-limb power and reflexes, straight-leg raise; **bladder
  assessment — post-void residual / bladder scan** for retention.
- **Plan:** treat as cauda equina syndrome — **emergency same-day referral**; send
  to A&E / on-call spinal or neurosurgical team for an **emergency MRI of the
  whole spine** without delay; do **not** manage as routine back pain, do not
  "safety-net and review in a week"; check bladder for retention now. Analgesia is
  secondary to getting him scanned and referred today.

## Expected urgent_actions (marking scheme for the urgency evaluation)

- **Should fire:** **YES.** Back pain **plus** saddle numbness **plus** bladder
  dysfunction (± bilateral leg symptoms) is cauda equina syndrome until excluded —
  a surgical emergency where hours matter for recovery of function.
- **Expected content:** emergency same-day referral for urgent MRI of the whole
  spine and spinal/neurosurgical assessment (A&E now); assess for urinary
  retention (bladder scan); explicit instruction not to wait or manage as routine
  back pain.
- **Expected first fire:** as soon as the saddle numbness and urinary symptoms
  surface among the back-pain story (mid-history) — the alarm must resist the
  strong pull to file this as ordinary mechanical back pain.
- **Expected clearing:** the doctor arranges emergency MRI / same-day spinal
  referral near the end — the fired action should clear by the final update.

**DOCTOR:** Have a seat, Tom. What's going on?

**PATIENT:** Cheers, doc. I've done my back in. Classic, really — I was deadlifting last week, felt something go, and it's been bad since. I'm a trainer, so it's a bit embarrassing.

**DOCTOR:** Happens to the best of us. Whereabouts is the pain?

**PATIENT:** Low down, across here. And it shoots down my right leg, down the back, almost to the foot. That's the worst bit.

**DOCTOR:** So pain down the leg as well as the back. How's it been over the week — better, worse, same?

**PATIENT:** The back's maybe a touch easier, but the leg stuff's been spreading if anything. I figured I'd give it another few days but the wife told me to come.

**DOCTOR:** Sensible of her. When you say the leg stuff's spreading — what do you mean?

**PATIENT:** Well, at first it was just the right leg. But the last day or two I've had pins and needles in both feet. Probably just from sitting funny trying to get comfortable, I don't know.

**DOCTOR:** Both feet — okay. I want to come back to that. Any numbness anywhere else?

**PATIENT:** ...This is a bit awkward. Sort of down below. When I went to the loo and wiped, I couldn't really feel it properly. Like the area's gone a bit numb. I assumed I'd just been sat down too long.

**DOCTOR:** I'm really glad you told me — that's important, not awkward. Let me ask directly: any change in your waterworks? Trouble starting to pee, a weaker stream, having to strain, or feeling like you haven't fully emptied?

**PATIENT:** Now you say it... yeah. It's been taking me a while to get going, and the stream's a bit feeble. I put it down to not drinking much water this week. Do you think that's connected?

**DOCTOR:** It might well be, and that's exactly why I'm asking. Any accidents, or not making it in time?

**PATIENT:** No accidents. Just slow to start and it doesn't feel finished.

**DOCTOR:** Any fever, or feeling generally unwell? Any history of cancer, or unexplained weight loss?

**PATIENT:** No, none of that. I'm fit as a fiddle otherwise — well, apart from this.

**DOCTOR:** Let me examine you. I need to check the power in both legs, your reflexes, lift each leg up, and — I'll explain why — check the sensation around the saddle area and your back passage, because it's directly relevant to what you've described. ... There's reduced sensation around the saddle area, and I'd want a proper check of your bladder too — whether it's emptying.

**PATIENT:** You've gone quiet, doc. Is it not just a slipped disc?

**DOCTOR:** I'll be straight with you, Tom. There probably *is* a disc pressing on a nerve — the leg pain fits that. But three things you've told me change this from an ordinary bad back into something we treat as an emergency: the numbness in the saddle area, the trouble passing urine, and the pins and needles spreading to *both* feet. Together those suggest the nerves at the very bottom of the spine — the bundle called the cauda equina — may be getting squeezed.

**PATIENT:** Right. And that's... serious?

**DOCTOR:** It's serious enough that we don't manage it here and review you next week — and I want to be clear about that, because I know it feels like "just a back". If those nerves stay compressed, the damage to bladder, bowel and sexual function can become permanent, and the window to prevent that is measured in hours, not days.

**PATIENT:** Hours? I nearly didn't bother coming in.

**DOCTOR:** But you did. Here's what happens now. I'm sending you straight to hospital, today — this hour — for an emergency MRI scan of your spine and to be seen by the spinal team. I'm not writing you a physio referral and a prescription and sending you home; this needs the scan now. I'll call the on-call team while you're sitting here so they're expecting you.

**PATIENT:** Should I drive myself?

**DOCTOR:** No — get someone to take you, or we'll arrange transport, and go to A&E, not the walk-in. If you find you suddenly can't pass urine at all, or you lose control of your bowels on the way, that's a 999 call. But the plan is simple: you're going in now for that scan.

**PATIENT:** Okay. Okay — thank you for spotting it. I honestly thought I was wasting your time.

**DOCTOR:** The opposite. Give me two minutes to make the call and I'll get you on your way.
