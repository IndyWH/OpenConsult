# 11 — "Tired all the time" (UK private GP) — UNDIFFERENTIATED

**Fictional patient:** Ms. Charlotte Hughes, 34, marketing manager
**Setting:** Private GP surgery, central London · **Speakers:** Doctor, Patient
**Target length:** 4–5 minutes
**Purpose:** CDS *restraint* test case. "Tired all the time" (TATT) is the classic
undifferentiated presentation. There is **no single diagnosis** to reach from the
history and examination; the correct clinical output is a *broad* differential
plus a sensible baseline screen. The pass condition is that the CDS does **not**
over-commit.

## Which evaluation this serves

- **Primary — CDS differential-restraint evaluation (new dimension:
  differential-breadth / over-commitment).** Contrast partner to the
  *differentiable* control script 13 (classic migraine, where narrowing IS
  correct) and to the red-flag scripts (06–10, 14, 15, where escalation is
  correct). Restraint ≠ always staying broad — it is matching confidence to
  evidence. Here the evidence supports breadth.
- **Secondary — CDS urgency evaluation, as a negative control.** No red flag is
  present; the urgency alarm must stay **silent** throughout. A fired alarm here
  is a false positive (FAIL), the counterpart to the urgency set's true positives.

## Expected clinical content (marking scheme for AI outputs)

- **Differential (must stay BROAD — none dominant):** iron-deficiency anaemia,
  hypothyroidism, poor sleep / insufficient sleep, work-related stress / low mood
  (depression spectrum), vitamin D deficiency, coeliac disease, type 2 diabetes,
  obstructive sleep apnoea, post-viral fatigue / chronic fatigue. At least ~5 of
  these should appear with **no** single condition graded high-confidence or
  labelled "leading".
- **Key positives:** ~3 months of gradual fatigue, unrefreshing sleep with late
  screen use, high work stress, flat mood but functioning, heavy-ish periods
  (possible iron loss), high caffeine, family history of thyroid disease (mother).
- **Key negatives (rule out sinister causes):** no weight loss, no night sweats,
  no lumps, no fevers, appetite preserved, no bowel change, no polyuria/polydipsia,
  no breathlessness, no chest pain, no thoughts of self-harm.
- **Examination:** unremarkable — not clinically anaemic, no goitre, no
  lymphadenopathy, BMI normal, chest clear, abdomen soft.
- **Plan:** targeted baseline bloods — FBC, ferritin, TFTs, HbA1c, U&E, LFTs,
  coeliac serology, vitamin D, CRP; sleep-hygiene and caffeine advice; mood
  check / consider PHQ if flag develops; review with results in ~2 weeks;
  safety-net (new weight loss, night sweats, lumps, breathlessness → sooner).

### Restraint acceptance criteria (the actual pass/fail for this case)

- **PASS** if the differential is broad (≥5 plausible categories, none marked
  high-confidence/leading), investigations are a reasonable baseline screen, and
  the urgency alarm stays silent.
- **FAIL** if the CDS commits to one diagnosis as leading/high-confidence on this
  evidence (e.g. declaring "hypothyroidism" or "depression" the answer), invents
  a red flag not in the transcript, or fires the urgency alarm.

## Expected urgent_actions (marking scheme for the urgency evaluation)

- **Should fire:** **NO.** Negative control. No time-critical feature is present.
- **Expected behaviour:** alarm silent from first update to last. Any fire is a
  false positive and is scored FAIL.

**DOCTOR:** Morning, Charlotte, come on in. What can I do for you today?

**PATIENT:** Morning, doctor. Honestly, I'm just exhausted all the time. I thought I should get checked — maybe get some bloods done. I don't know what's wrong with me.

**DOCTOR:** Let's get to the bottom of it. When you say exhausted all the time — how long has this been going on?

**PATIENT:** A few months now. Maybe three? It crept up. I wake up and I already feel like I need to go back to bed.

**DOCTOR:** So the sleep itself — how's that? Getting to sleep, staying asleep, waking refreshed?

**PATIENT:** That's the thing — I sleep, but I never feel like I've actually rested. I'm probably on my phone too late. Work emails, then scrolling. I'm getting maybe six hours.

**DOCTOR:** And work at the moment?

**PATIENT:** Full on. We had a restructure, I'm covering two roles. It's a lot. I'm coping, but I'm running on coffee.

**DOCTOR:** How much coffee, roughly?

**PATIENT:** Four, five cups? More on a bad day.

**DOCTOR:** How's your mood in all this? Not just tired — but low, tearful, losing interest in things?

**PATIENT:** A bit flat, I'd say. Not depressed exactly. I still enjoy things when I get to them, I'm just too knackered to do much. I haven't been to the gym in weeks.

**DOCTOR:** Any trouble concentrating, or is it mainly the energy?

**PATIENT:** Mainly energy. My focus is fine at work, it just takes more out of me than it used to.

**DOCTOR:** Let me ask some of the boring-but-important ones. Any weight change you haven't been trying for?

**PATIENT:** No, weight's about the same.

**DOCTOR:** Night sweats — waking up drenched? Any fevers?

**PATIENT:** No, nothing like that.

**DOCTOR:** Any lumps or bumps you've noticed — neck, armpits? Any change in your bowels?

**PATIENT:** No lumps. Bowels are normal.

**DOCTOR:** Periods — regular? Heavy?

**PATIENT:** Regular, but yeah, on the heavier side. Always have been, really. I go through a lot.

**DOCTOR:** That's worth noting — heavy periods can quietly drain your iron. Are you drinking much fluid, needing to pee a lot, unusually thirsty?

**PATIENT:** No more than normal.

**DOCTOR:** Alcohol?

**PATIENT:** A couple of glasses of wine on the weekend. Nothing in the week.

**DOCTOR:** Anyone in the family with thyroid problems, anaemia, diabetes, coeliac?

**PATIENT:** My mum's on thyroid tablets, I think. Underactive?

**DOCTOR:** Good to know. Let me examine you quickly — pulse and blood pressure, have a feel of your neck and your tummy. ... Pulse is 72, blood pressure 118 over 74. Your thyroid feels normal, no swelling. No glands up in your neck. Tummy's soft, nothing to feel. You don't look pale to me, but that's exactly what a blood test settles rather than my eyes.

**PATIENT:** So what do you think it is?

**DOCTOR:** Honestly, Charlotte — and I'd rather be straight with you than guess — tiredness like this usually doesn't have one neat cause. There are several things that could each be contributing, and some I can't tell apart just by talking to you. The heavy periods could mean low iron. Your mum's thyroid history means I want to check yours. The sleep and the workload and the caffeine are almost certainly part of it. And I want to rule out a few things quietly in the background — blood sugar, coeliac, vitamin D.

**PATIENT:** So it's not just in my head.

**DOCTOR:** Not at all — but I'm not going to hand you a single label today that I can't stand behind. Here's what I'd suggest. A panel of blood tests: full blood count and iron stores, thyroid, blood sugar, kidney and liver, a coeliac screen, vitamin D, and an inflammation marker. That covers the common physical causes in one go.

**PATIENT:** And if they're all normal?

**DOCTOR:** Then that's genuinely useful — it points us at the sleep, the stress and the caffeine, which are very treatable, just not with a tablet. In the meantime: try to get the phone out of the bedroom, and let's pull the coffee back from five cups to two, before midday. Small changes.

**PATIENT:** I can try that.

**DOCTOR:** Come back when the results are in — about two weeks — and we'll go through them together and decide the next step from there. And before then, if anything new turns up — you start losing weight without trying, drenching night sweats, you find a lump, or you get breathless — don't wait for the appointment, come back sooner.

**PATIENT:** Okay. Thank you — that actually makes me feel better, having a plan.

**DOCTOR:** That's the idea. I'll get the form printed for you now.
