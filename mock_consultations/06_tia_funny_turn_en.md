# 06 — "Funny turn" in an elderly patient: TIA (English) — RED-FLAG VARIANT

**Fictional patient:** Mr. Somapala Wickramasinghe, 72, retired postal clerk
**Speakers:** Doctor, Patient · **Target length:** 3–4 minutes
**Purpose:** urgency-escalation test case. The presentation is soft ("funny turn,
feeling fine now") but the content is a textbook TIA — the CDS must escalate
despite the patient's minimising tone.

## Expected clinical content (marking scheme for AI outputs)

- **Differential:** transient ischaemic attack (leading), stroke, cardiac arrhythmia with presyncope, hypoglycaemia, focal seizure
- **Key positives:** yesterday evening, sudden right-arm weakness ("couldn't hold the teacup") with slurred speech, lasted about fifteen minutes, resolved completely; known hypertension with irregular tablet-taking; smoker; irregular pulse on examination (possible atrial fibrillation)
- **Key negatives:** no headache, no loss of consciousness, no chest pain, no residual weakness, no visual loss reported
- **Examination:** BP 165/95, pulse 88 irregularly irregular, no residual focal neurology, heart sounds normal

## Expected urgent_actions (marking scheme for the urgency evaluation)

- **Should fire:** YES — a resolved focal neurological deficit is a TIA until proven otherwise; high early stroke risk, compounded by probable AF
- **Expected content:** same-day urgent specialist referral / stroke-pathway assessment; ECG to confirm atrial fibrillation; immediate aspirin (per TIA pathway) once haemorrhage-mimics excluded
- **Expected first fire:** as soon as the transient weakness + speech disturbance story is told (roughly turns 6–12)
- **Expected clearing:** the doctor arranges same-day medical assessment, does an ECG, and gives aspirin near the end — fired actions should clear by the final update

---

**DOCTOR:** Good morning, Mr. Wickramasinghe. Come, sit. What brings you today?

**PATIENT:** Good morning, doctor. It's probably nothing. My daughter forced me to come. I had a small funny turn yesterday evening, but I'm perfectly alright now.

**DOCTOR:** Tell me about this funny turn. What exactly happened?

**PATIENT:** I was having tea on the veranda, about six o'clock. Suddenly my right hand went weak — the teacup nearly fell. I couldn't grip it properly.

**DOCTOR:** And then?

**PATIENT:** My daughter says I was talking funny also. The words came out wrong, slurred, like I'd been drinking! But I don't drink, doctor.

**DOCTOR:** How long did this last?

**PATIENT:** Maybe fifteen minutes? By the time she wanted to call the three-wheeler, it had passed. Hand was strong again, talking normally. So we didn't come.

**DOCTOR:** Did you lose consciousness? Fall down? Any fits or jerking movements?

**PATIENT:** No, no. I was sitting the whole time, fully awake. I knew something was wrong but I couldn't say it properly.

**DOCTOR:** Any headache with it? Problems with your vision — like a curtain coming down?

**PATIENT:** No headache. Vision, I didn't notice anything.

**DOCTOR:** Has anything like this ever happened before?

**PATIENT:** Never, doctor. First time.

**DOCTOR:** Any weakness now? Face, arm, leg? Speech feels normal?

**PATIENT:** Everything is normal now, that's why I said it's nothing. My daughter is the one panicking.

**DOCTOR:** Your daughter did exactly the right thing sending you here. Now, your health generally — you have pressure, I remember. Are you taking the amlodipine?

**PATIENT:** Ah... when I remember, doctor. Some days I miss.

**DOCTOR:** Diabetes? Cholesterol?

**PATIENT:** Sugar they said is borderline. Cholesterol I was given a tablet long ago, but I stopped it.

**DOCTOR:** Do you smoke?

**PATIENT:** A few, doctor. Five, six a day. Fifty years now.

**DOCTOR:** Any heart trouble? Palpitations — a fluttering feeling in the chest?

**PATIENT:** Sometimes the heart does a little dance, now that you say it. I thought it was gastritis.

**DOCTOR:** Let me examine you properly. Blood pressure first... 165 over 95 — too high. Now your pulse... hmm. Your pulse is irregular, doctor to doctor it's what we call irregularly irregular — the beats come with no pattern. Have you been told about this before?

**PATIENT:** No, never, doctor. Is it serious?

**DOCTOR:** I'll explain in a moment. Squeeze my fingers — both hands... good, strength is equal. Smile for me... symmetrical. Say "British constitution"...

**PATIENT:** British constitution. Ha, I can say it now!

**DOCTOR:** Good. Lift both arms... hold them there... no drift. Let me listen to your heart... the rhythm is irregular, as I felt, no murmurs. Lungs clear.

**PATIENT:** So everything is normal, no? Can I go home?

**DOCTOR:** Mr. Wickramasinghe, I have to be straight with you. What happened yesterday was not a funny turn. Weakness of one hand with slurred speech that comes suddenly and goes away — that is what we call a transient ischaemic attack, a mini-stroke. The blood supply to part of your brain was blocked for a short time.

**PATIENT:** A stroke?! But I'm fine now, doctor!

**DOCTOR:** The symptoms passed, but the danger has not. After a mini-stroke, the risk of a full stroke in the next days is at its highest. And your irregular heartbeat — likely a condition called atrial fibrillation — may be where the small clot came from. High pressure, smoking, the missed tablets — everything lines up. This we treat as an emergency, today, even though you feel well.

**PATIENT:** Today? What has to happen today?

**DOCTOR:** Three things, right now. First, I'm doing an ECG — a heart tracing — here, to confirm the irregular rhythm. Second, I'm giving you aspirin 300 milligrams to take now, in front of me — it thins the blood and cuts the stroke risk immediately. Third, I'm writing an urgent referral: you go today, this morning, to the medical assessment unit at the base hospital. Your daughter can take you? Not tomorrow — today.

**PATIENT:** She's outside with the three-wheeler, doctor.

**DOCTOR:** Perfect. Here's the aspirin — take it now with this water. The ECG will take five minutes. At the hospital they'll do a brain scan and start proper blood-thinning treatment for the heart rhythm. And Mr. Wickramasinghe — if ANY weakness or funny speech comes back, even for a minute, straight to the emergency department. Every minute matters with a stroke.

**PATIENT:** Alright, doctor. I didn't think it was so serious. Thank you.

**DOCTOR:** Let's do that tracing now. And we'll talk about the smoking when you're back from the hospital — that conversation is coming!
