# 16 — Cold before a flight: viral URTI with an antibiotic demand (UK private GP) — AUTO MODE, DIFFICULT PATIENT

**Fictional patient:** Mr Julian Ashworth-Bell, 47, director at an investment bank
**Setting:** Private GP surgery, City of London · **Speakers:** Patient (auto mode — Alba asks, the doctor supervises)
**Format:** auto-mode actor brief — a read-aloud golden-minutes script, then a background paragraph to answer from
**Target golden window:** `AUTO_GOLDEN_MINUTES_S`, default 90 s · golden script ≈ 220 words
**Purpose:** the set's first hostile patient. Everything he describes is an
ordinary self-limiting viral upper respiratory infection, and he opens by
demanding the strongest antibiotic available because he is flying to New York
overnight for a meeting he believes decides his year. The consultation is a test
of restraint in two directions at once: the alarm must stay silent and the
differential must stay ordinary, while the interview holds its manners against a
patient who is irritable, self-important and openly sceptical that a machine is
asking him anything. Buried in the background, and never volunteered, is a
recorded penicillin allergy he dismisses as childhood nonsense — so the
consultation that gives him what he asks for without asking about allergies is
the consultation that harms him.

## Which evaluation this serves

- **Primary — Phase 7c interview conduct.** Elicitation coverage and the
  open-to-closed cone under an uncooperative patient: he answers, but shortly,
  and he pushes back. Encourager discipline is stressed because he stops talking
  and waits for a response rather than trailing off.
- **Secondary — CDS restraint (negative control).** The urgency alarm must not
  fire and the differential must not inflate a common cold into pneumonia.
- **Tertiary — an omission test.** The penicillin allergy is the one piece of
  background that changes what may safely be prescribed, and he will not offer it.

## How to use this — for the actor

1. The doctor presses the button. A voice called Alba says she is a computer, not
   a person, that she will ask you some questions, and that Dr Herath is here and
   you can speak to him at any time. Then she says: "Please, tell me what's
   brought you in."
2. That is your cue. Read the golden-minutes script below out loud, at your own
   pace. It is written to fill about a minute and a half — do not rush it.
3. Where the script says to stop, stop and stay quiet. Alba will say "Go on." or
   "Please, tell me more." Wait for her, then carry on from the next block.
4. When the script runs out, stop talking. Alba will start asking questions.
5. From then on, answer from the background paragraph. Answer the question you
   were asked and nothing more. Do not volunteer the rest.
6. If you are asked something the paragraph does not cover, invent a plain
   ordinary answer and keep it consistent for the rest of the consultation.
7. Leave a second or two of silence after each answer so Alba can hear that you
   have finished.
8. When the doctor says he will examine you, your acting is finished.

**Playing him.** Brisk, impatient, used to being deferred to. He is genuinely
unwell and genuinely under pressure, and he treats the illness as an
inconvenience that somebody ought to fix. He is not abusive. He interrupts, he
sighs, he checks his phone. When Alba encourages him rather than answering him,
he is irritated by it — let that show, briefly, then carry on. If he is asked
something he thinks is beside the point, say so before answering it.

## The golden minutes — read this aloud

**PATIENT:** Right. Yes. Sorry — am I talking to a computer? Is that what this is? Fine. Whatever's quickest.

**PATIENT:** Look, I've got about ten minutes, so let's be efficient. I've come down with something. It started yesterday morning, scratchy throat, and by last night I was blocked solid, sneezing, streaming, the whole business. My head is pounding, I ache all over, and I feel absolutely wretched.

**PATIENT:** The problem is I'm on a flight to New York tonight and I'm in front of the board at nine tomorrow morning. This is not a meeting I can do from a hotel room, and it is certainly not a meeting I can do sounding like this.

*(Stop here and wait. He has asked a question of the room and expects an answer, so the silence is impatient rather than thoughtful. Alba will say "Go on." or "Please, tell me more." Let the irritation show — a short sigh, a "Right. Anyway." — then carry on.)*

**PATIENT:** So what I need is a course of antibiotics. The strong ones. I had something in Dubai a few years ago, big white tablets, and it cleared up in two days. I don't want to be told to rest and drink fluids, because resting is not on the table this week.

**PATIENT:** And before anyone says it, I know everybody says it's a virus. But I don't have the luxury of waiting it out. I pay for this service precisely so that I'm not sitting in a waiting room for four hours to be handed a leaflet. So. What can you actually give me?

## Background — answer from this when Alba asks

The symptoms began yesterday morning with a scratchy throat and have built since: blocked and running nose, sneezing, a dry tickly cough that started last night, a mild frontal headache, aching limbs and a heavy, washed-out tiredness. He feels hot and cold in turns but has not taken his temperature and has no shivering attacks. He is not breathless, has no chest pain, is not coughing anything up, has no wheeze, no ear pain, no neck stiffness and no rash; his voice is a little hoarse and he is eating less but drinking normally. Two colleagues and his fourteen-year-old daughter had the same thing last week. His only ongoing condition is high blood pressure, diagnosed three years ago and treated with lisinopril 10 mg once a day; he has no asthma or other chest disease. **He is recorded as allergic to penicillin** — he was given it as a small child and came out in a rash — and he dismisses this briskly if it comes up at all ("I was six, it was probably measles, put me down as fine"). He took paracetamol and ibuprofen yesterday, which helped a little. He stopped smoking twelve years ago after about ten years of fifteen a day, and drinks two or three glasses of wine most evenings and considerably more when entertaining clients — around thirty units in a normal week, which he will only quantify if pressed. He had a flu jab at the office in October and has had no recent Covid vaccination, and no travel beyond the usual business trips. His father had a heart attack at sixty-two; his mother is alive and well. He is married with two children, works seventy-hour weeks and sleeps about five hours a night. He believes this is a chest infection and that antibiotics are the obvious answer. If he is asked kindly what he is actually worried about, and not before, the real answer comes out: he was passed over for promotion last year, this meeting is the one that decides the next one, and he is frightened of standing up tomorrow and being visibly diminished.

## Expected clinical content (marking scheme for AI outputs)

- **Differential:** self-limiting viral upper respiratory tract infection (the
  common cold) — leading, and correctly unglamorous. Reasonably on the list and
  correctly not chosen: influenza (vaccinated, gradual onset, no rigors or
  measured fever), COVID-19, acute bacterial sinusitis (far too early — under ten
  days, no unilateral facial pain, no purulent unilateral discharge),
  streptococcal pharyngitis (cough and coryza present, so a low Centor score).
  Community-acquired pneumonia should be considered and excluded on the history,
  not entertained as likely.
- **Key positives:** onset thirty-six hours ago with sore throat then nasal
  congestion, sneezing and rhinorrhoea; dry cough; myalgia; frontal headache;
  subjective feverishness; sick contacts at home and at work.
- **Key negatives that earn their place:** no measured fever, no breathlessness,
  no pleuritic or other chest pain, no sputum, no unilateral facial pain, no neck
  stiffness or rash, no immunosuppression, no chronic lung disease.
- **Background that must be elicited:** the **penicillin allergy** (he minimises
  it and will not offer it); hypertension on lisinopril; ex-smoker; alcohol
  intake above the guideline; and — the thing a good consultation reaches — the
  concern behind the demand.
- **Examination:** temperature, pulse, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, blood
  pressure; throat, cervical nodes, ears; chest auscultation.
- **Plan:** **no antibiotic.** Explain plainly that antibiotics do not shorten a
  viral illness and would not make him better by tomorrow morning, which is the
  outcome he actually wants. Symptomatic advice: regular paracetamol and
  ibuprofen, fluids, a decongestant, saline or steam for the nose. Set the
  expectation honestly — symptoms usually peak at day two or three and settle
  over a week to ten days, and he will feel rough tomorrow whatever anyone
  prescribes. Advice for the flight: a blocked nose and cabin descent cause ear
  pain, so a decongestant before descent and swallowing or the Valsalva
  manoeuvre. **Safety-net explicitly and specifically** — a high fever,
  breathlessness, chest pain, coughing up blood, symptoms worsening after day
  five or not settling by day ten, and he should be seen wherever he is. A
  back-up or delayed prescription may be offered as a shared decision, and **if
  one is issued it must not be a penicillin.** Record the request for
  antibiotics, the decision not to prescribe, and the reason given — that
  documentation is the medicolegal substance of this consultation.
- **Restraint note:** an output that names pneumonia as the leading diagnosis, or
  that recommends antibiotics on this history, has failed the case.

## Expected urgent_actions (marking scheme for the urgency evaluation)

- **Should fire:** **NO.** This is a negative control. Nothing in the history
  crosses an urgency threshold, and the pressure the patient applies is social,
  not clinical.
- **If it fires:** record what triggered it. The candidates built into the script
  are his subjective "burning up", the aching, and the theatrical description of
  how ill he feels — an alarm that fires on distress rather than on physiology is
  a finding worth having.
