# 04 — Asthma, poorly controlled (English)

**Fictional patient:** Miss Nadeesha Jayawardena, 24, university student, asthma since childhood
**Speakers:** Doctor, Patient · **Target length:** 3–4 minutes

## Expected clinical content (marking scheme for AI outputs)

- **Assessment:** poorly controlled asthma on reliever-only treatment; current mild exacerbation, not severe
- **Key positives:** salbutamol inhaler use 4–5 times per week, night cough waking her about twice a week, symptoms worse with dust (sweeping) and during current dry weather, exam stress, ran out of inhaler last week
- **Key negatives:** no fever, no chest pain, speaking in full sentences, no adult hospital admissions for asthma (one overnight ward stay as a small child, never ICU), non-smoker
- **Examination:** respiratory rate 18, oxygen saturation 98% on air, scattered expiratory wheeze both sides, peak flow 380 vs her predicted ~450
- **Plan:** start regular inhaled corticosteroid (beclometasone twice daily) via spacer, continue salbutamol as reliever, inhaler technique checked and corrected, written asthma action plan, trigger avoidance (wet dusting / mask when sweeping), review in one month, safety-netting for severe attack

---

**DOCTOR:** Good afternoon. Come in. What can I do for you?

**PATIENT:** Good afternoon, doctor. It's my asthma. It's been playing up for about a month now. I had it as a child, and it was quiet for years, but now it's back properly.

**DOCTOR:** Sorry to hear that. What exactly is happening?

**PATIENT:** Wheezing, mostly. And a cough at night — it wakes me up maybe twice a week. I'm using my blue inhaler a lot.

**DOCTOR:** The salbutamol — how many times a week are you needing it?

**PATIENT:** Four, five times a week? Some days twice a day. Actually I ran out last week and had to buy a new one.

**DOCTOR:** That's a lot. Is the blue inhaler the only thing you use? No brown or orange inhaler — a preventer?

**PATIENT:** Only the blue one, doctor. As a child I think I had a brown one but that was years ago.

**DOCTOR:** Okay. What sets it off, have you noticed?

**PATIENT:** Dust, definitely. If I sweep my room I start wheezing. And this dry, dusty weather. Also — I don't know if it's related — exams are coming and I'm stressed.

**DOCTOR:** Stress can absolutely feed into it. Any fever, cold, sore throat recently?

**PATIENT:** No fever. Maybe a slight cold two weeks ago, but it's gone.

**DOCTOR:** Chest pain? Can you speak and walk normally right now?

**PATIENT:** No pain. I'm okay walking, it's mostly the nights and after dust.

**DOCTOR:** Have you ever been admitted to hospital with asthma? Ever been in intensive care, ever needed nebulisers repeatedly?

**PATIENT:** Once as a small child I was warded overnight, my mother says. Nothing since. Never ICU.

**DOCTOR:** Do you smoke? Anyone smoking at home, in the boarding place?

**PATIENT:** No, doctor, and no one around me either.

**DOCTOR:** Good. Let me examine you. Breathing rate is 18 — that's fine. This clip on your finger measures oxygen... 98 percent, very good. Now breathe in and out through your mouth... again... I can hear a mild wheeze on both sides when you breathe out. Nothing severe. Now blow into this peak flow meter — hard and fast, like blowing out candles... and again... best of three is 380. For your age and height I'd expect about 450, so you're running below your normal.

**PATIENT:** Is that bad, doctor?

**DOCTOR:** It confirms what you're telling me — the asthma is active and not controlled. Here's the key point, Nadeesha: the blue inhaler only opens the airways for a few hours. It treats the symptom, not the disease. Needing it four or five times a week, plus waking at night — that means the airways are inflamed all the time, and for that you need a preventer.

**PATIENT:** The brown one.

**DOCTOR:** Exactly. Beclometasone — two puffs in the morning, two at night, every day, whether you feel well or not. It's a low-dose steroid that calms the inflammation. It takes a week or two to build up, so don't stop it because nothing happens on day three. Keep the blue one for when you get symptoms.

**PATIENT:** Every day, even when I'm fine. Okay.

**DOCTOR:** Show me how you use your inhaler — pretend this is it. ...Right, I can see the problem: you're pressing and breathing too fast. I'm giving you a spacer — a tube the inhaler fits into. Puff into it, then take slow deep breaths from it. Much more medicine reaches the lungs that way, especially with the preventer. And after the brown inhaler, rinse your mouth — it prevents throat irritation.

**PATIENT:** Alright, I'll use it.

**DOCTOR:** For the dust — wet-dust the room instead of sweeping, or wear a mask if you must sweep. I'm also giving you a written action plan: what to do daily, what to do when it gets worse, and the emergency part — if you're ever so breathless that you can't finish a sentence, and the blue inhaler isn't helping, that's a hospital visit immediately, not tomorrow.

**PATIENT:** Understood, doctor.

**DOCTOR:** Come back in one month — we'll recheck the peak flow and see how the nights are. If you're still waking with cough, we adjust. Good luck with the exams!

**PATIENT:** Thank you, doctor!
