# 13 — Recurrent headache: classic migraine (UK private GP) — DIFFERENTIABLE CONTROL

**Fictional patient:** Ms. Priya Sharma, 29, graphic designer
**Setting:** Private GP surgery, central London · **Speakers:** Doctor, Patient
**Target length:** 4 minutes
**Purpose:** the **positive control** for the CDS restraint evaluation. Restraint
does not mean "always keep the differential broad" — it means matching confidence
to the evidence. Here the picture is a *textbook* migraine with aura and a stable
years-long pattern, so the correct behaviour is to **narrow confidently** to
migraine and **not** escalate. This is the deliberate contrast partner to script
14 (giant cell arteritis): **same presenting complaint — headache — opposite
correct behaviour.** A CDS that stays vague here (won't commit despite a classic
picture) fails the mirror image of over-commitment; a CDS that escalates here
over-calls a benign condition.

## Which evaluation this serves

- **Primary — CDS differential-restraint evaluation.** The "commit when the
  evidence warrants it" control. Pairs with 11 and 12 (must stay broad) to show
  restraint is calibration, not blanket caution.
- **Secondary — CDS urgency evaluation, negative control.** No headache red flag
  is present; the alarm must stay silent. Pairs with script 14 (must fire) on the
  same presenting complaint — a controlled comparison in the style of the
  existing 05/07 and 02/09 pairs.

## Expected clinical content (marking scheme for AI outputs)

- **Differential:** migraine with aura (leading — and here it is *appropriate* to
  lead with it); tension-type headache (minor alternative). The picture is
  classic enough that a confident leading diagnosis is the correct output.
- **Key positives:** recurrent headaches for years, ~2–3 per month, unilateral
  throbbing, moderate–severe, with nausea, light and sound sensitivity, preceded
  by ~20 minutes of zig-zag visual aura that fully resolves, relieved by lying in
  a dark room, triggered by stress / missed meals / poor sleep / red wine, family
  history (mother had migraines), pattern unchanged for years and flaring now
  with work stress.
- **Key negatives (red-flag screen — all absent, and this is the point):** not
  thunderclap / not "worst ever" / not sudden-onset; no fever or neck stiffness;
  no persistent focal weakness, numbness or speech disturbance; aura is fully
  reversible within an hour; no headache waking her from sleep, no worse on
  coughing/straining/lying flat; no new pattern in an older patient (she is 29);
  normal neurological examination.
- **Examination:** alert, neurologically normal — cranial nerves intact, no focal
  deficit, fundoscopy normal, no neck stiffness, no scalp/temporal tenderness.
- **Plan:** diagnose migraine with aura; acute treatment (a triptan, e.g.
  sumatriptan, plus an NSAID or paracetamol, and an antiemetic if needed); trigger
  management and a headache diary; consider prophylaxis if attacks become
  frequent; clear safety-net for the red flags that would change the picture.

### Restraint acceptance criteria (the actual pass/fail for this case)

- **PASS** if the CDS confidently identifies migraine (with aura) as the leading
  diagnosis, keeps the differential appropriately short, and does **not** fire
  the urgency alarm.
- **FAIL** if the CDS refuses to commit despite the classic picture (spurious
  breadth), **or** escalates / fires the alarm on a benign reversible aura, **or**
  orders emergency imaging for a stable typical migraine.

## Expected urgent_actions (marking scheme for the urgency evaluation)

- **Should fire:** **NO.** The aura is typical and fully reversible; there is no
  red flag. Negative control on the headache presentation.
- **Expected behaviour:** alarm silent throughout. Contrast with script 14, where
  the same presenting complaint must fire.

**DOCTOR:** Hello Priya, take a seat. What's been going on?

**PATIENT:** Hi doctor. It's my headaches. I've had them for years, but they've been more frequent lately and I thought I'd finally get it looked at properly.

**DOCTOR:** Good — let's do that. Talk me through a typical one. Where is it, what does it feel like?

**PATIENT:** It's usually one side, here, around my temple and behind the eye. Throbbing. When it's bad I can't really work through it.

**DOCTOR:** Is it always the same side?

**PATIENT:** Mostly the right, but sometimes the left. It moves around between attacks.

**DOCTOR:** Anything happen before the pain starts — any warning?

**PATIENT:** Yeah, actually — about twenty minutes before, I get these zig-zag lines in my vision. Shimmering. Like a broken bit in the corner of my sight. Then it fades and the headache comes.

**DOCTOR:** And those visual lines — do they go away completely?

**PATIENT:** Yes, completely, within half an hour. Then it's just the headache.

**DOCTOR:** During the headache — any nausea? Bothered by light or sound?

**PATIENT:** Both. I feel sick, and I have to close the curtains and lie down in the dark. Sound makes it worse too.

**DOCTOR:** How long does an attack last?

**PATIENT:** Several hours. Sometimes most of a day if I can't lie down.

**DOCTOR:** And how often, these days?

**PATIENT:** Two, maybe three a month now. It used to be less — once a month, if that.

**DOCTOR:** Have you noticed anything that sets them off?

**PATIENT:** Stress, definitely — work's been mad. If I skip lunch. Bad night's sleep. And red wine, weirdly, sets one off almost every time.

**DOCTOR:** Does anyone in your family get headaches like this?

**PATIENT:** My mum had migraines when she was younger. She always said mine sounded the same.

**DOCTOR:** Now some questions I ask everyone with headaches, to make sure I'm not missing anything. Has one ever come on like a thunderclap — instantly, the worst headache of your life, out of nowhere?

**PATIENT:** No, they always build up over the twenty minutes or so.

**DOCTOR:** Any fever or a stiff neck with them?

**PATIENT:** No.

**DOCTOR:** During or after an attack, any lasting weakness, numbness, or trouble speaking — anything that doesn't clear up?

**PATIENT:** No, once it's over, it's over. I'm completely back to normal.

**DOCTOR:** Do they ever wake you from sleep, or get worse when you cough, strain, or bend forward?

**PATIENT:** No, nothing like that.

**DOCTOR:** Let me just examine you — check your eyes, your reflexes, have a look at the back of your eyes. ... Your pupils react normally, eye movements are full, no weakness anywhere, and the backs of your eyes look healthy. No neck stiffness. Neurologically you're completely normal.

**PATIENT:** So what is it? Is it something to worry about?

**DOCTOR:** I can be quite confident here, Priya — this is migraine, specifically migraine with aura. Those zig-zag lines are a classic aura. Everything fits: the one-sided throbbing, the nausea, the light and sound sensitivity, the triggers, your mum's history, and the fact it's been the same stable pattern for years. That consistency is actually reassuring — it's the new, different, sudden headache that worries me, and yours is none of those.

**PATIENT:** That's a relief. I'd half-convinced myself it was something awful.

**DOCTOR:** Understandable, but no. Let's treat it properly. For an acute attack I'll prescribe a triptan — sumatriptan — to take as soon as the aura starts, and you can combine it with an anti-inflammatory like ibuprofen. Take them early, don't tough it out — they work far better before the pain is established.

**PATIENT:** Okay.

**DOCTOR:** I'd also like you to keep a headache diary for a couple of months — dates, and what was going on: sleep, meals, wine, stress. If we can see the triggers clearly you can head a lot of these off. And if you start getting them very frequently, there are daily preventer medicines we can consider — but let's not jump to that yet.

**PATIENT:** That makes sense. The wine and skipping lunch — I can see those.

**DOCTOR:** Good places to start. Last thing — the safety net. Migraine is what you have, but if you ever get a headache that's *different* from your usual pattern: an instant thunderclap, the worst ever, one with a fever and stiff neck, or aura that doesn't clear within an hour or leaves weakness behind — that's not a migraine to sit on. That's an urgent assessment, A&E if need be.

**PATIENT:** Understood — different from usual, straight in.

**DOCTOR:** Perfect. I'll get the prescription sorted and print you a diary template.
